Your Identity Is Not a Dataset.

AI systems can transform the way images, voices and personal information are created, analyzed and shared. These capabilities can support accessibility, creativity and research, but they also raise serious questions when people do not know how their information is being used.
Consent is more than clicking “agree.” People need understandable information about what is collected, why it is needed, how long it may be kept, and whether it may be used to train or improve a system. The answers depend on the product, its terms and the laws that apply.

A photo is not permission for everything.

A photo can reveal more than a person’s face. It may show location clues, school or workplace details, other people in the frame or private information in the background. When an image is uploaded to an online service, how it is processed depends on that service’s features, settings and terms.

Before uploading, look for the service’s explanation of data retention, deletion, model improvement and whether content may be reviewed by people. Do not assume every product follows the same policy, and check the current policy rather than relying on old screenshots.

Consent also involves people other than the account holder. If an image identifies a friend, classmate or family member, ask before uploading it for a transformation that they may not expect.

Questions to investigate: What data is collected? What is the stated purpose? Can a user delete it?

When a face becomes raw material.

Generative systems can create or alter images, audio and video. Misuse can include impersonation, deceptive fabricated media or non-consensual manipulation of someone’s likeness. The technology alone does not tell us whether a particular clip is real; verification requires context and evidence.
When documenting an incident, find the earliest credible report, check whether the affected person or an institution has commented, and distinguish allegations from confirmed findings. Avoid republishing humiliating or intimate material: repeating it can cause further harm.
Questions for a case study: What is independently verified? Who was affected? What did the platform do? What protections or reporting routes were available? What remains unknown?

Bias is a measurable system risk.

The US National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) evaluated face-recognition algorithms and reported demographic differences in many of the algorithms tested. NIST also explains that performance depends on the algorithm, task and data; the findings should not be flattened into a claim that every system behaves identically.
The ethical implication is practical: systems used in consequential settings need testing across relevant groups, clear documentation and a process for challenging errors. A vendor’s broad accuracy claim is not a substitute for evidence about a particular deployment.

A Human-centred approach to generative AI.

UNESCO’s guidance for generative AI in education and research advocates a human-centred approach. It discusses data privacy, age-appropriate use and the need for institutions to develop policies and capacity as tools evolve.
The guidance does not say that every use of AI should be prohibited. Instead, it frames questions about how systems are validated, how people’s data is protected, and how educational use can remain appropriate and meaningful.
For a school or family policy, useful discussion points include: what tools are allowed, what information must never be entered, what age restrictions apply, when an adult should be involved, and how students can report a concern.
